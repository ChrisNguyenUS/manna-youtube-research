# Target Positioning Doc — Maya Chen, Reset Physio

## 1. The business behind the content

Reset Physio sells one thing: a 12-week, one-on-one rehab and strength program for runners, delivered in the Austin clinic with a weekly check-in between sessions. It costs $2,400, paid up front or in three installments. Nobody arrives through insurance or a doctor's referral. They find the clinic on Instagram, book a free 20-minute assessment, and roughly one in three of those assessments turns into a program.

That matters for content because the viewer and the buyer are the same person. There is no gatekeeper — no referring physician, no HR department, no spouse who has to sign off on a $150 co-pay. The runner watches, the runner decides, the runner pays. So every video can speak directly to the runner, and the job of every video is narrow: make an injured runner believe that *this* physio would actually figure out what's wrong with them, when the last two didn't. Views from healthy runners, coaches, or other physios are pleasant but they don't feed the business. Trust from an injured runner does.

## 2. Ideal Viewer Avatar — Comeback Chris

Chris is 38. He's a marketing manager at a mid-sized software company, married, one kid, and he ran his first marathon at 33 mostly on a dare. He finished, cried a little at the line, and has been hooked ever since. He runs four days a week before work, tracks everything on a Garmin, and has a foam roller under the couch that he uses about once a month and feels guilty about the rest of the time.

For the last eighteen months, his right knee has flared up past mile ten. Not every run — that's the maddening part — but reliably on the long ones, and always at the point in a training block where he's finally feeling fit. He's deferred one race already. He has another registration sitting in his inbox and a quiet fear that he's going to have to defer that one too, and that at some point deferring turns into quitting.

He goes looking for help at night, usually after a long run went badly. He searches "knee pain after 10 miles running," reads three contradictory articles, watches a YouTube mobility routine, does it for four days, and stops when nothing changes. He's done two rounds of physical therapy through insurance. Both handed him a sheet of clamshells and bridges. Neither watched him run.

**What Chris believes**
- Rest is the only thing that actually works, and it doesn't work for long.
- Physios just hand you a sheet of exercises and bill your insurance.
- Strength training makes runners slow and heavy.
- If it were a real injury, an MRI would have found it by now.

**What Chris fears**
- Being told to stop running for good.
- Spending another $150 on a session that changes nothing.
- Deferring the race a second time and admitting he's "not a runner anymore."
- That the problem is his age, not his mechanics.

**What Chris has already tried**
- Two rounds of insurance PT (clamshells, bridges, "come back in two weeks")
- YouTube mobility and "runner's knee" routines
- New shoes, twice, on a shoe-store employee's advice
- A chiropractor, three visits
- Taking three weeks off, then having it come right back

**What Chris complains about**
- "Nobody actually watched me run."
- "I did the exercises and it still hurts."
- "Every answer online contradicts the last one."
- "They treated the knee. I don't think it's the knee."

Chris is the target rather than "runners" or even "injured runners" because Chris is the one who pays $2,400. A college cross-country kid with the same knee has a trainer. A casual 5K runner stops running. A 38-year-old with a marathon registration, a Garmin full of data, and eighteen months of failed fixes is the person who will spend real money on the physio who finally looks at the whole picture.

## 3. Dream outcome

**Cross the finish line of his next marathon pain-free, and know he can keep running into his 50s.**

The gap between where Chris is and that outcome isn't information — he's consumed more running content than most physios. The gap is that nobody has looked at *him*. Every fix he's tried was generic: a routine for "runner's knee," shoes for "overpronators," rest for "overuse." His knee is a symptom of something upstream — hip strength, cadence, a loading pattern that breaks down when he's tired — and no one has watched him run long enough to see it. That's what every video is really about: the difference between treating the knee and finding the cause.

## 4. Pain point bank

Ranked by degree of pain. Most painful first.

1. **"Why does my knee only hurt after mile 10?"** — tactical, pain 5. This is the exact thing he types into Google at 11pm. It's specific, it's his, and the fact that it's *only* after mile 10 is the clue nobody's explained to him. Heard in: search, DMs.
2. **"Do I actually have to stop running to heal?"** — broad, pain 5. The fear underneath everything. Heard in: the first two minutes of every assessment call.
3. **"Why did two rounds of PT not fix this?"** — broad, pain 4. He's angry about the money and the time, and ready to hear that the approach was wrong, not him. Heard in: DMs, comments on competitor posts.
4. **"Should I run through pain or rest until it's gone?"** — tactical, pain 4. He gets both answers from different sources and is currently doing neither well. Heard in: comments.
5. **"Is it my shoes, my form, or my hips?"** — tactical, pain 3. He suspects it's not the shoes but has no way to check. Heard in: DMs.
6. **"How much strength work does a runner really need?"** — broad, pain 3. Tied to the belief that lifting makes him slow. Heard in: comments.
7. **"Can I still hit my goal time after an injury?"** — broad, pain 3. Identity question dressed as a training question. Heard in: sales calls, late.
8. **"What should a return-to-running week look like?"** — tactical, pain 2. He'll want this once he trusts her, not before. Heard in: after they've booked.

*Add to this forever. Source: comments — yours and competitors' — and DMs.* Pain is the raw material of a hook. The most painful question, packaged so Chris recognizes it as *his* question in the first second, is the video that stops the scroll. The eighth item on this list is a fine video; the first is a video that gets him to book.

## 5. Target Authority Statement

Three shapes:

- "I help injured runners with getting back to running." *(too vague — "injured runners" is every runner eventually, and "getting back" has no stakes)*
- "I help runners in their 30s and 40s with knee and hip injuries that keep coming back." *(closer — names the recurrence, which is his real frustration, but loses the outcome)*
- **"I help injured marathoners get back to pain-free running without giving up the race."**

The third wins because it names the person (injured marathoners — not runners, not athletes), the outcome (pain-free running), and the fear it removes (giving up the race). "Without giving up the race" is the part Chris hears. It tells him this physio understands that deferring is the thing he's actually scared of, and that the plan is built around not having to.

Said out loud, in a bio or a video intro:
- "I'm the physio for marathoners who've been told to just rest."
- "I get injured runners back to the start line — without the sheet of clamshells."
- "If your knee only hurts after mile 10, this account is for you."

## 6. Unique positioning hypothesis

*Hypothesis from self-knowledge — not yet validated against competitors.*

| Attribute | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Topic | Strong | Return-to-running for injured marathoners is far narrower than the generic "runner's knee" content that dominates the space. |
| Substance | Strong | Twelve years treating runners plus two marathon comebacks of her own; she can show gait video and explain what she's seeing, not just prescribe exercises. |
| Avatar | Strong | One person, one situation: the 30s–40s marathoner scared of deferring again. |
| Format | Possible | Filming real gait assessments with real patients is unusual in the niche, but she hasn't tested it on camera yet. |
| Visuals | No | Standard clinic setting, nothing distinctive on screen yet. |

**The lane.** The position Maya could own is "the physio who watches you run." Most physio and running content is filmed in a gym or on a mat: here are five exercises for runner's knee, here's a hip mobility flow. Maya can post something almost nobody else does — real gait breakdowns of real comeback runners, with her drawing on the footage and explaining why the knee hurts at mile 10 and what it has to do with the hip. Tied to one promise, back to the start line pain-free, that's a topic lane, a substance lane, and an avatar lane at once. The Format attribute becomes strong the moment she films three of these and one works. Visuals stay weak until she decides on something repeatable — the same treadmill, the same angle, the same on-screen annotation style — that makes a Reset Physio video recognizable before she says a word.

## 7. The one thing

*When someone needs to get back to running after an injury, I want to be the first name they think of.*

The words to own in Chris's head are **"comeback"** and **"watch you run."** Not "rehab," not "physio," not "mobility." If a runner in Austin hears "comeback plan" and thinks of Reset Physio, or thinks "I want someone who'll actually watch me run" and Maya's face comes to mind, the positioning has worked.

## 8. Ask before every video

**Would this be worth Comeback Chris's time?**
**Would it make him trust me more?**

Three ideas that pass both, pulled from the bank:

- **"Your knee hurts at mile 10 because of what your hip does at mile 8."** A gait breakdown of a real patient, slowed down, with the moment the hip drops circled. Pain point 1, in her own footage.
- **"Two rounds of PT didn't fix it. Here's what they missed."** Not an attack on other physios — a plain explanation of why a clamshell sheet can't fix a loading problem that only shows up when you're tired. Pain point 3.
- **"Run through it or rest? Here's the test I use."** A 30-second decision rule Chris can use tonight. Pain point 4, and it earns trust by giving away something useful.
